Parts of the boat

Directions and hull

Bow / Stern
Front / back of the boat. Forward and aft are the directions toward each.
Port / Starboard
Left / right side when facing forward. Port is red, starboard is green. (Port and red both have fewer letters.)
Windward / Leeward
The side toward the wind / the side away from it. Leeward is said "LOO-ard."
Hull, Deck, Cockpit
The body of the boat, its top surface, and the recessed area where the crew sits and steers.
Transom
The flat back surface of the stern.
Beam
The widest part of the boat. "Abeam" means directly off the side.
Keel
The weighted fin under the hull. It keeps the boat upright (counteracts heeling) and resists sliding sideways (leeway).
Rudder, Tiller, Helm
The rudder is the underwater blade that steers. The tiller (or wheel) turns it. The helm is the steering station; the helmsman steers.
Lifelines, Stanchions
Wire railings around the deck and the posts that hold them.

Rigging

Standing rigging
Fixed wires that hold the mast up. Forestay (to the bow), backstay (to the stern), shrouds (to the sides). Spreaders are struts that hold the shrouds out.
Running rigging
Lines you adjust while sailing.
Halyard
Raises (hoists) a sail.
Sheet
Controls a sail's angle to the wind. Mainsheet, jib sheet.
Boom
The horizontal spar along the bottom of the mainsail. Attached to the mast at the gooseneck.
Boom vang
Pulls the boom down and keeps it from lifting.
Outhaul
Pulls the clew of the mainsail aft along the boom to flatten the foot.
Topping lift
Holds the boom up when the mainsail is lowered.
Cleat, Winch
A fitting to secure a line; a drum that gives mechanical advantage when trimming.
Traveler
A track that moves the mainsheet's attachment point side to side.

Parts of a sail

Head
Top corner.
Tack
Lower forward corner.
Clew
Lower aft corner (where the sheet attaches on a jib, the outhaul on a main).
Luff
Forward edge. Also a verb: a sail is luffing when it flaps along this edge.
Leech
Aft edge.
Foot
Bottom edge.
Battens
Stiffeners in pockets along the leech of the main.
Telltales
Yarn or ribbon on the sail (and shrouds) that show airflow.
Jib / Genoa / Main
The headsail forward of the mast (a genoa is a large jib that overlaps the mast); the mainsail is behind the mast on the boom.

Points of sail

A point of sail is the boat's direction relative to the wind. Wind is named for where it comes from: a north wind blows from the north.

Turn toward the wind (trim sails in).
Bear away / bear off / fall off
Turn away from the wind (ease sails out).
Port tack / starboard tack
Named for the side the wind comes over. The boom is on the opposite side. Boom on starboard means you're on port tack.
By the lee
Running with the wind slightly on the same side as the boom. Risk of an accidental jibe.

Tacking and jibing

Tacking (coming about) turns the bow through the wind. Jibing turns the stern through the wind.

StepTackJibe
Helm warns"Ready about""Prepare to jibe"
Crew confirms"Ready""Ready"
Helm turns"Helm's a-lee" (or "Hard a-lee," "Tacking"), push tiller to leeward"Jibe-ho," pull tiller to windward
Jibe safely. The boom swings across with force. Trim the mainsheet in before the stern passes through the wind, keep heads low, then ease out on the new side. An accidental jibe happens when running by the lee.

In irons: stopped head-to-wind with sails luffing. To get out, back the jib: hold it out to one side, and the wind pushes the bow toward the other side. Once you're turned onto a close reach, release the jib to the normal side, trim both sails, and sail off. If the boat starts drifting backward, the steering reverses, so steer the stern the way you want it to go.

Sail trim

  • Basic rule: ease the sail out until the luff just starts to flutter, then trim in until it stops. "When in doubt, let it out."
  • Head up → trim in. Bear away → ease out.
  • Jib telltales: both should stream straight back.
    • Windward telltale flutters → sail is too loose for your course. Trim in or bear away.
    • Leeward telltale flutters → sail is overtrimmed. Ease out or head up.
    • Tiller tip: push the tiller toward the misbehaving telltale.
  • Too much heel in a gust: ease the mainsheet, or head up slightly to depower. In strong wind, reef (reduce sail area).
  • Weather helm: the boat wants to turn toward the wind, often from too much heel or mainsail. Lee helm is the opposite.

Rules of the road

The Navigation Rules (COLREGS internationally, Inland Rules in US inland waters) use two roles. The give-way vessel must take early and substantial action to keep clear. The stand-on vessel must hold course and speed, but must act if collision can't be avoided by the give-way vessel alone. Every vessel must avoid a collision (General Prudential Rule).

Two sailboats

  1. Opposite tacks: the port-tack boat gives way to the starboard-tack boat.
  2. Same tack: the windward boat gives way to the leeward boat.
  3. If you're on port tack and can't tell which tack a boat to windward is on, keep clear.

Rules that apply to everyone

  • Overtaking: the boat overtaking keeps clear, no matter what kind of boat. A sailboat overtaking a powerboat must give way.
  • Narrow channels: a sailboat (or any boat under 20 m) must not impede a vessel that can only navigate safely within the channel. Same in traffic separation schemes.
  • Under engine = power: a sailboat motoring, even with sails up, is a power-driven vessel.
  • Risk of collision: if the other boat's compass bearing stays steady while the distance closes, you're on a collision course.

Pecking order (who gives way to whom)

Each vessel gives way to the ones above it, except when overtaking.

  1. Not under command (NUC: breakdown, can't maneuver)
  2. Restricted in ability to maneuver (RAM: dredging, towing, laying cable)
  3. Constrained by draft (CBD, international rules)
  4. Engaged in fishing (nets, trawls; not trolling a line)
  5. Sailing vessel
  6. Power-driven vessel

Two power-driven vessels

  • Head-on: both turn to starboard and pass port to port.
  • Crossing: the vessel that has the other on its starboard side gives way.

AnchoredOne all-round white light.
Sailboat under 7 mShould show sidelights and sternlight if practicable; if not, must have a white flashlight or lantern ready to show in time to prevent a collision.

Sidelights shine 112.5° (dead ahead to just aft of the beam), the sternlight 135° astern. If you see a red light you're looking at a vessel's port side; green, its starboard side; red and green together, it's coming straight at you; only white, you're probably seeing its stern (or a boat at anchor).

Day shapes: a cone, point down, means a sailboat motorsailing. A black ball means anchored.

Charts and units

  • Nautical mile = 1 minute of latitude ≈ 6,076 ft (about 1.15 statute miles).
  • Knot = one nautical mile per hour. (Never "knots per hour.")
  • Measure distance on the latitude scale along the side of the chart, never the longitude scale along the top.
  • The chart title block says whether soundings (depths) are in feet, fathoms (6 ft) or meters.
  • The compass rose shows true north (outer ring) and magnetic north (inner ring); the difference is variation.
  • A lee shore is a shore downwind of you. It's dangerous because the wind pushes you toward it.

Knots

Bowline
A fixed loop at the end of a line that won't slip and is easy to untie. Used to attach jib sheets to the clew.
Cleat hitch
Secures a line to a horn cleat: a turn around the base, a figure-eight over the horns, a locking hitch.
Figure-eight
A stopper knot at the end of a sheet so it can't run out through a block.
Square (reef) knot
Joins two ends of the same line, as when tying reef points. Not for critical loads.
Clove hitch
Quick hitch around a post or rail, often for fenders. Can slip if not under steady load.
Round turn & two half hitches
Secures a line to a post, ring or piling; holds under load.
Sheet bend
Joins two lines, especially of different diameters.

Safety equipment (US Coast Guard)

  • Life jackets (PFDs): one USCG-approved wearable PFD of the right size for each person aboard, in good condition and readily accessible. Boats 16 ft and longer also need a throwable device (Type IV: ring or cushion). Newer labels show a performance level instead of a "Type," but older Type-labeled PFDs are still legal.
  • Children: under federal rules, children under 13 must wear a life jacket on a moving recreational boat unless they're below deck or in an enclosed cabin. Your state may set a different age.
  • Visual distress signals (flares, orange flag, distress light): required on coastal waters for boats 16 ft and up; boats under 16 ft on coastal waters need night signals when out between sunset and sunrise. On most inland lakes and rivers federal rules don't require them, though some states do.
  • Sound-producing device: boats under 12 m need some way to make an efficient sound signal (a whistle or air horn). At 12 m and up a whistle is required; at 20 m and up, a bell as well.
  • Fire extinguisher: required on boats with inboard engines, enclosed engine or fuel spaces, closed living spaces, or permanent fuel tanks. Since 2022, disposable (non-rechargeable) extinguishers must be replaced 12 years after the date of manufacture.
  • Ventilation and backfire flame arrester: required on boats with gasoline inboard engines.
  • Navigation lights between sunset and sunrise and in restricted visibility.
  • Registration and numbers as your state requires.

VHF radio (channel 16 for distress and calling)

  • Mayday: grave and imminent danger to life or vessel.
  • Pan-pan ("pahn-pahn"): urgent, but no immediate danger to life.
  • Sécurité ("say-curitay"): safety or navigation information.

Health

  • Hypothermia: get the person out of the water and wind, remove wet clothes, insulate, warm gradually. Wear a PFD; cold water exhausts you fast.
  • Heat and sun: hydrate, shade, sunscreen.
  • Seasickness: stay on deck, look at the horizon, take the helm, stay hydrated.

Crew overboard

  1. Shout "Crew overboard!"
  2. Throw flotation immediately (throwable cushion or ring).
  3. Assign a spotter who points at the person and never looks away.
  4. Press the GPS MOB button if you have one.
  5. Return using a recovery maneuver and stop alongside the person.

Two recovery methods

  • Quick-stop: head up into the wind right away, tack and leave the jib backed, then circle around and come back to the person. Keeps the boat close to them.
  • Figure-eight: sail away on a beam reach a few boat lengths, tack, bear away below the person, then come back on a close reach, luffing sails to slow and stop beside them.

Why the final approach is on a close reach: you can speed up or slow down just by trimming or easing the sails.

Weather

  • Check the forecast before you go: NOAA Weather Radio (VHF WX channels), apps, local reports. Know your limits for wind strength.
  • Small craft advisory: conditions hazardous to small boats.
  • Thunderstorm signs: tall, dark, anvil-topped cumulonimbus clouds, sudden temperature drop, sudden wind shift or gusts, a falling barometer. Head in early.
  • Sea breeze: on sunny days, land heats up and the breeze off the water builds in the afternoon.
  • Fog: slow down, sound fog signals, show lights, keep a lookout.
  • Gusts show as dark patches ("cat's paws") moving across the water toward you.

Docking and anchoring

Docking

  • Rig fenders and dock lines before you arrive.
  • Approach slowly, ideally into the wind or current so they help you stop. "Never approach a dock faster than you're willing to hit it."
  • Bow line and stern line hold the ends; spring lines run diagonally and keep the boat from moving forward and aft along the dock.
  • Crew step off (never jump) and never use hands or feet to fend off between the boat and dock.

Anchoring

  • Pick a spot with good holding (sand, mud), enough room to swing, and protection from the wind.
  • Approach head to wind, stop, lower the anchor (don't throw it), let the boat drift back as you pay out rode.
  • Scope = length of rode ÷ (water depth + height of bow above water). Use about 7:1 for normal conditions (5:1 minimum with chain).
  • Example: 10 ft deep, bow 5 ft high, 7:1 scope → 15 × 7 = 105 ft of rode.
  • Set the anchor by gently backing down (or letting the wind push you back) until it holds. Check landmarks to make sure you're not dragging.
  • Common anchors: Danforth (fluke), plow, claw (Bruce), mushroom (for moorings).
