Reading the forecast

Check the forecast the night before and again before you leave the dock. Look at the wind and how it changes through the day, since the afternoon is often very different from the morning.

Where to get it

  • Your lake: most inland lakes and reservoirs don't get an NWS marine forecast. Use the NWS point forecast (click your spot on the lake at weather.gov) and its hourly weather graph, which shows sustained wind, gusts, and thunderstorm chances hour by hour. Watch for a Lake Wind Advisory, which many southern NWS offices issue for strong winds on area lakes.
  • NOAA Weather Radio on the VHF weather channels (WX1 to WX7, 162.400 to 162.550 MHz), or any weather radio. It broadcasts warnings for your county as they're issued.
  • Radar on your phone before and during the sail. On summer afternoons it's the best way to see storms building nearby.
  • NWS marine forecasts at weather.gov/marine cover coastal waters, the Great Lakes, and a few large southern lakes such as Lake Pontchartrain and Lake Okeechobee. You'll use these for coastal sailing in ASA 103.
  • Apps and models such as Windy show wind maps from forecast models. They're useful for timing and trends; treat the NWS forecast and any warnings as the official word.

How to read the wording

Sample lake-day point forecast.
TODAY Sunny, high near 91. South wind 8 to 13 mph, with gusts as high as 22 mph. 30 percent chance of showers and thunderstorms after 2pm.
TONIGHT Mostly clear. South wind around 5 mph.

Converted to knots, that's about 7 to 11 kt gusting 19, which is fine for a morning sail but a reef-or-go-home afternoon, with a real chance of a pop-up storm after 2 pm. Plan to be back at the dock by early afternoon.

Sample coastal marine forecast (the format you'll see in ASA 103).
TODAY SW winds 10 to 15 kt, increasing to 15 to 20 kt with gusts to 25 kt in the afternoon. Seas 2 to 3 ft. Chance of showers and tstms late.
Direction
Where the wind blows from. A SW wind comes from the southwest.
Speed
Marine forecasts use knots. Public (land) forecasts use mph. 1 knot ≈ 1.15 mph, so 20 mph is about 17 kt.
Gusts
Brief peaks above the sustained wind. Plan your sail area for the gusts, not the average.
Seas
Combined height of wind waves and swell, given as significant wave height: the average of the highest third of the waves. Expect occasional waves up to about twice that.
Wave period
Seconds between crests. A short period with a given height means steep, choppy waves; a long period means gentler swells.
Becoming, increasing
A change during the period. "Becoming NW" after a W wind is a clockwise shift, often a sign a front has passed.

Weather on southern lakes

Lake sailing in the southern US has its own rhythm. These are the patterns to plan around.

Through the year

Summer
Hot, humid, often light wind in the morning. Heating builds puffy cumulus by late morning, and pop-up thunderstorms are common from early afternoon into evening. They form fast, sometimes right over the lake, with little warning from the forecast. Sail early.
Spring
The windiest season and the main severe-weather season. Strong south winds ahead of cold fronts, then squall lines and severe storms as the fronts pass.
Fall
Often the best sailing: cold fronts bring clear, dry air and steady north winds behind them. In Texas and the southern Plains a strong one is called a blue norther: the wind can swing to the north and jump to 25 kt or more within minutes as the temperature drops.
Winter
Good breeze with each passing front, but cold water. Dress for falling in, and remember sea smoke and fog on cold mornings.
Hurricane season
June to November. Tropical storms and their remnants can bring strong wind and flooding rain hundreds of miles inland.

How wind behaves on a lake

  • Shifty and puffy. Wind coming off land is broken up by trees, hills, and buildings, so expect bigger, more frequent shifts and gusts than on open water. Watch for dark patches moving across the water.
  • Wind shadows along a tree-lined or high shore. The breeze can drop to nothing near the windward shore and fill in well offshore.
  • Funneling. Wind speeds up when it's squeezed through a gap, down a valley, or along a long cove or arm of the lake.
  • Fetch. The longer the stretch of open water the wind blows across, the bigger the chop. The downwind end of a long reach of lake can be rough when the upwind end is flat.
  • Daily cycle. On sunny days the wind often builds from late morning through mid-afternoon and fades toward evening, as heating stirs stronger wind down from aloft.

Other lake hazards

  • Heat. Heat exhaustion and heat stroke are real risks on a summer lake. Carry plenty of water, shade, and sun protection.
  • Lightning is the biggest weather danger. The Southeast, and Florida especially, has the most lightning in the country.
  • Lake levels. Reservoir levels rise and fall with rain and dam releases. At low water, stumps, sandbars, and old roadbeds come close to the surface; check the current level against "full pool" before you go.

Marine products cover coastal waters, bays, the Great Lakes, and a few big lakes. On most inland lakes you'll see the land-based products instead: Lake Wind Advisory, Severe Thunderstorm Warning, Tornado Warning. Wind speeds below are sustained winds or frequent gusts. The ASA 103 test expects you to know the marine set.

Wind strength and the Beaufort scale

The Beaufort scale ties wind speed to what the water looks like, so you can estimate the wind by eye. Whitecaps begin to appear at around 8 to 10 knots.

The scale continues to force 12 (hurricane, 64+ kt). As a rough guide for a 20 to 26 ft keelboat with a newer crew: force 2 to 3 is ideal for practice, force 4 is lively and often the time to reef, and force 5 and up is a day to stay in until you have more experience. Your instructor's limits for your boat come first.

Wind force grows fast. The force of wind on a sail rises with the square of its speed. Twenty knots pushes about four times as hard as ten.

Pressure, wind, and the barometer

  • Air flows from high pressure toward low. In the Northern Hemisphere, wind circles clockwise around a high and counterclockwise around a low, angled slightly in toward the low.
  • Isobars are lines of equal pressure on a weather map. Close together means strong wind; far apart means light wind.
  • Buys Ballot's law: in the Northern Hemisphere, stand with your back to the wind and the low is on your left, slightly ahead.
  • Standard sea-level pressure is 1013 millibars (29.92 inches of mercury).
  • Watch the trend, not the number. A fall of 3 mb or more in 3 hours signals strengthening wind and worsening weather. A fast rise after a front can bring strong, gusty wind too.
  • Veering is a clockwise wind shift (S to W). Backing is counterclockwise (W to S). In the Northern Hemisphere the wind usually veers as a front passes.

Sea breezes and land breezes

On the coast and on very large lakes, heating sets up a daily breeze cycle. Most inland lakes are too small for a true lake breeze, but the same heating is what makes their afternoon wind gusty. You'll need this for ASA 103 on the coast.

  • A sea breeze is strongest on hot, sunny days when the overall (gradient) wind is light. It often reaches 10 to 15 kt or more by mid-afternoon on a morning that started calm.
  • Signs it's coming: a line of puffy cumulus building over the shoreline, a dark band of ripples moving in from open water.
  • In the Northern Hemisphere a sea breeze often veers (shifts clockwise) as the afternoon goes on.
  • A strong offshore gradient wind can block the sea breeze entirely; a light offshore wind may delay it, then it fills in.
  • Large lakes get the same effect, called a lake breeze. Near hills and valleys, air flows uphill by day and downhill at night, which can bring sudden gusts off high ground.
  • Practical upshot: on a summer day, a calm morning can turn into a breezy, choppy afternoon. Plan the return trip for it.

Fronts

A front is the boundary between two air masses. Most changes in sailing weather arrive with one.

  • In mid-latitudes weather generally moves west to east, which is why "red sky at night, sailor's delight; red sky in morning, sailors take warning" has some truth to it.
  • "Mares' tails and mackerel scales make tall ships carry low sails": streaky cirrus and rippled altocumulus often run ahead of an approaching system.

Thunderstorms and squalls

Thunderstorms are the most common serious weather hazard for small-boat sailors, and on southern lakes they're a near-daily summer risk. Air-mass storms grow from fair-weather cumulus in an hour or less and can send outflow gusts of 30 kt or more across water that was calm minutes before.

Signs one is coming

  • Cumulus clouds building tall and dark, topping out in a flat anvil (cumulonimbus).
  • A low, dark shelf or roll cloud along the leading edge.
  • Static on AM radio, distant thunder, lightning.
  • A sudden drop in temperature and a wind shift. The gust front (outflow) often arrives before the rain, can come from a completely different direction, and can be much stronger than the breeze you had.
  • A Special Marine Warning on the VHF weather channel.

Flash to bang

Sound travels about 1 statute mile in 5 seconds. Count the seconds from the lightning flash to the thunder and divide by 5. If you can hear thunder at all, you're close enough to be struck.

What to do

  1. Head in early if you can make shelter before it arrives. Storms often move at 10 to 25 knots, faster than you can sail.
  2. If you can't, get sea room: move away from a lee shore and other boats.
  3. Everyone in life jackets. Close hatches, stow loose gear.
  4. Reduce sail before the wind hits: reef deeply or drop the main and sail under jib alone, or motor.
  5. Note your position and which way is safe water.
  6. Stay low in the cockpit, away from the mast, shrouds, and other metal. Don't touch two metal fittings at once.
  7. Wait about 30 minutes after the last thunder before relaxing.

Squall line: a line of thunderstorms, often ahead of a cold front, that can stretch for miles with no gap to sail around. Waterspouts are tornadoes over water; move away at right angles to their path.

Fog

Fog forms when air cools to its dew point and the moisture condenses. When the forecast air temperature and dew point are within a few degrees, expect fog or haze.

Radiation fog
Forms on clear, calm nights as the land cools. Drifts onto rivers and harbors and usually burns off by late morning.
Advection fog
Warm, moist air moves over colder water (Maine, the California coast, the Great Lakes in spring). Can last for days and can persist in a fair breeze.
Sea smoke
Very cold air over much warmer water, so the water seems to steam. Common in late fall and winter.

In fog: slow to a safe speed, turn on navigation lights, sound the fog signal (one prolonged blast and two short every 2 minutes for a sailboat), post a lookout forward, hoist a radar reflector, and fix your position by GPS before the shore disappears. See the Navigation Rules module for the full set of fog signals.

When to reef

Reef when you first think about it. It's easier to shake out a reef in a lull than to tie one in during a gust. If the forecast calls for it, reef at the dock before you leave.

Signs it's time

  • The boat heels so far the rail is near the water and it keeps happening.
  • Strong weather helm: you have to pull the tiller hard to windward to hold course.
  • The boat rounds up into the wind in the gusts.
  • You're easing the mainsheet constantly just to stay upright.
  • Whitecaps are becoming frequent (force 4 and building), or a front or storm is on the way.

Tying in a slab (jiffy) reef

  1. Head up to close-hauled or heave to so the boat is steady.
  2. Ease the mainsheet and the vang; take up the topping lift so the boom doesn't drop.
  3. Lower the main halyard until the reef cringle at the luff reaches the boom, and hook it on the tack horn.
  4. Re-tension the halyard.
  5. Haul in the reef line to pull the leech cringle down and aft to the boom.
  6. Tie the reef points around the loose bundle of sail with reef knots, not around the boom.
  7. Trim the main and sail on.

You can also reduce sail at the front: roll in some of a furling jib or switch to a smaller headsail. Shaking out a reef is the same steps in reverse; untie the reef points first.

How the rules work

The Navigation Rules come in two sets. The International Rules (COLREGS, 1972) apply on the high seas and in connected waters. The Inland Rules apply inside the COLREGS demarcation lines, shown as dashed magenta lines across harbor entrances on the chart, including the Great Lakes and Western Rivers. They're nearly identical; this guide marks the differences that matter for a small boat. The USCG publishes both in one free book, "Navigation Rules and Regulations Handbook."

Key definitions (Rule 3)

Underway
Not at anchor, not made fast to the shore, and not aground. A drifting boat is underway.
Making way
Underway and moving through the water.
Power-driven vessel
Any vessel propelled by machinery, including a sailboat with its engine running.
Sailing vessel
Under sail with no engine in use.
Engaged in fishing
Using nets, lines, or trawls that restrict its ability to maneuver. A boat trolling lines is not "engaged in fishing."
Not under command (NUC)
Can't maneuver as the rules require because of some exceptional circumstance, such as a breakdown.
Restricted in ability to maneuver (RAM)
Its work limits its ability to get out of the way: dredging, laying cable, servicing a buoy, some towing, launching aircraft.
Constrained by draft (CBD)
International Rules only. A deep-draft ship that can't leave a channel. The Inland Rules have no CBD category.
Restricted visibility
Fog, mist, heavy rain, snow, or anything similar that reduces visibility.

Rules that come first

  • Rule 2, Responsibility: nothing in the rules excuses neglect of good seamanship. You may depart from a rule when that's needed to avoid immediate danger.
  • Rule 5, Lookout: every vessel must keep a proper lookout by sight and hearing (and radar, if fitted) at all times. On a small sailboat that includes checking under the jib.
  • Rule 6, Safe speed: always go at a speed that lets you stop or maneuver in time. Visibility, traffic, wind, sea, and current all count.
  • Rule 7, Risk of collision: if the compass bearing to an approaching vessel doesn't change appreciably while the range closes, risk of collision exists. If in doubt, assume it does. Large ships can be a risk even with a slowly changing bearing.
  • Rule 8, Action to avoid collision: make it early, positive, and large enough to be obvious to the other vessel. One big course change beats a series of small ones. Slowing or stopping is also an option.

Steering and sailing rules

Rule 18, who keeps out of whose way

Except when overtaking, or in a narrow channel or traffic scheme, each vessel keeps out of the way of those above it:

  1. Not under command
  2. Restricted in ability to maneuver
  3. Constrained by draft (International; others "shall avoid impeding" it)
  4. Engaged in fishing
  5. Sailing vessel
  6. Power-driven vessel

A seaplane on the water keeps clear of all of them.

Encounters

Rule 12, Sailboats
Opposite tacks: the port-tack boat keeps clear. Same tack: the windward boat keeps clear. If you're on port tack and can't tell what tack a boat to windward is on, keep clear. The windward side is the side opposite the main boom.
Rule 13, Overtaking
Any vessel overtaking another keeps clear, whatever the types. You're overtaking if you're coming up from more than 22.5° abaft the other vessel's beam; at night you'd see only its sternlight. Once overtaking, you stay the give-way vessel until you're past and clear.
Rule 14, Head-on
Two power-driven vessels meeting head-on (you see both sidelights, or the masthead lights in line) each alter course to starboard and pass port to port.
Rule 15, Crossing
Two power-driven vessels crossing: the one that has the other on its own starboard side keeps clear and should avoid crossing ahead.
Rule 16, Give-way
Take early and substantial action to keep well clear.
Rule 17, Stand-on
Hold course and speed. You may act once it's clear the give-way vessel isn't doing so, and you must act when collision can't be avoided by the give-way vessel alone. In a crossing, a power-driven stand-on vessel should avoid turning to port for a vessel on its own port side.
Power-driven mnemonics. "If to starboard red appear, 'tis your duty to keep clear." "Green to green or red to red, perfect safety, go ahead."

Channels and traffic schemes

  • Rule 9, Narrow channels: keep to the starboard side of the channel when it's safe. A sailing vessel or any vessel under 20 m must not impede a vessel that can only navigate safely inside the channel. Don't cross a channel if it impedes such a vessel. Don't anchor in a narrow channel.
  • Rule 10, Traffic separation schemes: these are one-way lanes for shipping off major ports. Cross a lane on a heading as close to a right angle as practical, and a sailboat or boat under 20 m must not impede ships following the lane.
  • "Not impede" means acting early so a ship never has to change course for you. It applies even when you'd otherwise be the stand-on vessel.

Conduct in restricted visibility (Rule 19)

When vessels can't see each other, there is no stand-on or give-way vessel. Rule 19 applies instead:

  • Every vessel proceeds at a safe speed for the conditions; power-driven vessels keep engines ready to maneuver at once.
  • On hearing a fog signal apparently forward of the beam, or when a close-quarters situation can't be avoided, reduce speed to the minimum needed to keep steering, and take all way off if necessary. Navigate with extreme caution until the danger is over.
  • If you detect a vessel by radar alone and a close-quarters situation is developing, act in ample time. Avoid turning to port for a vessel forward of the beam (unless it's being overtaken), and avoid turning toward a vessel abeam or abaft the beam.

Small boats

  • Sailboat under sail: sidelights and a sternlight. Under 20 m these can be combined in one tricolor at the masthead. Optional extra: all-round red over green at the masthead, but never together with the tricolor.
  • Sailboat under 7 m: show sailing lights if practicable; otherwise have a white flashlight or lantern ready to show in time to prevent a collision. The same goes for a boat under oars.

  • Inland only: law enforcement boats may show a flashing blue light. A barge being pushed ahead shows a special flashing yellow light at its bow.

Lights by vessel

What you'd actually see on a dark night. Tap the Practice quiz tab and choose "Night identification" to drill these.

Two masthead lights (range lights) mark a vessel 50 m or longer: the forward one is lower. When the two line up vertically, the ship is pointed straight at you. If the lights open up with the lower one to the left, the ship's bow is heading to your left.

Day shapes

Black shapes hoisted where they can best be seen show special status by day. Many match the night lights: a ball for each red light of NUC, the same red-white-red as ball-diamond-ball for RAM.

Overtaking

  • International (narrow channel only): two prolonged + one short, "I intend to overtake you on your starboard side"; two prolonged + two short, "on your port side." The vessel being overtaken agrees with prolonged, short, prolonged, short.
  • Inland: one short, "I intend to overtake you on your starboard side"; two short, "on your port side." The vessel being overtaken answers with the same signal to agree, or the danger signal if it's not safe.
  • Inland, vessels can agree by VHF radio instead of whistle signals.

Distress signals (Annex IV)

Any of these means a vessel is in distress and needs help:

  • Continuous sounding of any fog-signal device
  • A gun or explosive signal fired about once a minute
  • SOS in Morse code (··· ––– ···) by light or sound
  • "Mayday" spoken by radio, or a DSC distress alert
  • Red parachute rockets, red hand flares, or orange smoke
  • Flames on the vessel (for example a burning tar barrel)
  • Slowly and repeatedly raising and lowering outstretched arms
  • Code flags N over C, or a square flag with a ball above or below it
  • An orange flag with a black square and ball; dye marker; an EPIRB signal
  • Inland only: a high-intensity white light flashing 50 to 70 times a minute

Latitude and longitude

Latitude
Distance north or south of the equator, 0° to 90° N or S. Lines of latitude (parallels) run east–west. The latitude scale runs up the sides of the chart.
Longitude
Distance east or west of the prime meridian at Greenwich, 0° to 180° E or W. Lines of longitude (meridians) run north–south. The scale runs along the top and bottom. All of the US is west longitude.
Writing a position
Latitude first: 32° 47.5' N, 096° 48.2' W. One degree = 60 minutes ('). Minutes are usually given with tenths.
Distance comes from the latitude scale. One minute of latitude equals one nautical mile anywhere on earth. Minutes of longitude shrink toward the poles, so never measure distance on the top or bottom scale. Measure on the side scale, level with where you're working.

Reading the chart

  • Scale: a large-scale chart (1:20,000) shows a small area in great detail, like a harbor. A small-scale chart (1:1,200,000) shows a large area with little detail. Use the largest-scale chart available near hazards. Memory aid: large scale, large features.
  • Title block: tells you the area, the scale, and whether soundings are in feet, fathoms (6 ft), or meters. Always check it first.
  • Soundings are depths at the chart datum, in US tidal waters mean lower low water (MLLW), so most of the time you'll have a little more water than charted.
  • Heights of bridges and lights are measured above mean high water, so you'll usually have a little more clearance than charted.
  • Depth contours connect equal depths. Shallow water is tinted blue; land is buff; areas that cover and uncover with the tide are green.
  • Magenta marks lighted aids, restricted areas, and other things you most need to notice.
  • Hazards: rocks, wrecks, and obstructions have their own symbols; a dotted circle around one means it's a danger to navigation.
  • U.S. Chart No. 1 is the free key to every symbol and abbreviation on NOAA charts.
  • Updates: NOAA charts are printed on demand or used electronically; the Local Notice to Mariners lists changes such as moved buoys.

The compass, variation, and deviation

  • The compass rose on the chart has an outer ring for true north and an inner ring for magnetic north.
  • Variation is the angle between true and magnetic north at your location. It's printed in the center of the rose with its year and annual change, e.g. "VAR 3°45'W (2024) 6'E" (decreasing 6' a year).
  • Deviation is compass error caused by the boat's own iron and electronics. It changes with heading and comes from your boat's deviation card.
TVMDC. True, Variation, Magnetic, Deviation, Compass ("True Virgins Make Dull Companions"). Going from true toward compass, add west, subtract east ("east is least, west is best"). Going the other way, reverse it.

Example: true course 090°, variation 5° W, deviation 2° E. Magnetic = 090 + 5 = 095° M. Compass = 095 − 2 = 093° C.

Most small-boat sailors plot in magnetic, using the inner ring of the rose, so they can steer straight from the chart.

Plotting, bearings, and fixes

Parallel rules
Walk them from your course line to the compass rose (or the reverse) to read or lay down a direction.
Dividers
Measure distance by spanning a line, then setting the dividers on the latitude scale beside it.
Course line
Labeled with direction above (C 095 M) and speed below (S 5).
Bearing
The direction from you to an object, taken with a hand-bearing compass.
Line of position (LOP)
A line you know you're somewhere on. Plot a bearing as a line through the object. Label the time above and bearing below.
Fix
Where two or more LOPs cross. Pick objects about 90° apart for two bearings, or about 60° apart for three. Three bearings usually form a small triangle (a cocked hat); you're probably inside it. A fix is drawn as a circle with the time.
Range
Two charted objects lined up, one behind the other. A very accurate LOP needing no compass.
Danger bearing
A bearing line to a landmark that keeps you clear of a hazard. Stay on the safe side of it.

Dead reckoning

Dead reckoning (DR) is working out where you are from your course, speed, and time since your last fix. A DR position is drawn as a half circle with the time.

60 D = S T. Distance (nm) × 60 = speed (kt) × time (minutes). So D = S × T ÷ 60.
5 kt for 36 minutes: D = 5 × 36 ÷ 60 = 3 nm.
6 nm at 4 kt: T = 60 × 6 ÷ 4 = 90 minutes.

DR ignores current and leeway, so it drifts from the truth over time. An estimated position (a square) allows for them. Get a new fix whenever you can.

Tides and currents

Tide
The vertical rise and fall of the water. Most US coasts have two highs and two lows a day.
Current
Horizontal movement of water. Tidal current floods toward land and ebbs toward the sea. Slack is the pause between.
Set and drift
Set is the direction a current flows toward (the opposite of how wind is named). Drift is its speed in knots.
Rule of twelfths
Across the roughly six hours from low to high, the tide rises about 1/12, 2/12, 3/12, 3/12, 2/12, 1/12 of its range each hour. Fastest change mid-tide.
Tide and current tables
NOAA predicts tide heights and current times at reference stations. Wind and river flow can push real levels well off the prediction.

Inland lakes have no meaningful tide, but rivers have current, and reservoirs can have current near dam releases and inflows.

Lake maps

  • Most inland lakes aren't covered by NOAA charts. Sailors use lake maps from chart apps (Navionics and similar), the lake authority, or the Corps of Engineers.
  • Lake depths are often shown at a reference level such as normal or full pool. When the lake is 6 ft low, every depth is 6 ft less.
  • Reservoirs flooded old river channels, roads, and forest. The original river channel is the deep water; standing timber and stumps lurk on the flats and in coves.
  • Lake buoys follow the same US system: red and green lateral marks (red right returning means heading upstream or toward the head of the lake), and white regulatory buoys with orange shapes for danger, no-wake, and keep-out areas.
  • Learn the lake: note where the shallows, coves, and marinas are, and pick a few landmarks you can take bearings on.

Making a routine call

  1. Listen first so you don't talk over anyone.
  2. On 16 or 9: "Sea Breeze, Sea Breeze, this is Windward." (Say the other boat's name up to three times, then yours.)
  3. When they answer, propose a working channel: "Switch six-eight." Both switch and continue there.
  4. Keep it short. Say "over" when you expect a reply and "out" when you're done.

Phonetic alphabet for spelling: Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliett, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar, Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray, Yankee, Zulu.

Mayday
Grave and imminent danger to a vessel or person: sinking, fire, a person overboard you can't recover, a life-threatening medical emergency.
Pan-pan
Urgent but not immediately life-threatening: dismasted, engine failure drifting toward danger, a non-life-threatening injury.
Sécurité
Safety or navigation information, such as a big log adrift in the channel.

Each is said three times at the start of the call.

Making a Mayday call

Channel 16, high power. Speak slowly and clearly.

Mayday script
1. "MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY."
2. "This is Windward, Windward, Windward."
3. "MAYDAY, Windward."
4. Position: lat/long from the GPS, or a bearing and distance from a landmark.
5. Nature of distress: "We are taking on water and sinking."
6. Help needed: "Request immediate assistance."
7. Number aboard and injuries: "Three people aboard, all wearing life jackets."
8. Description: "26-foot sailboat, white hull, blue sail cover."
9. "Over."

If nobody answers, repeat. If you hear a Mayday and no one answers it, write down the details and relay it ("Mayday relay"). "Seelonce Mayday" means stay off the channel except for the emergency.

Crew overboard, in more depth

  1. Shout "Crew overboard!", throw flotation, appoint a spotter who points continuously, press the MOB button on the GPS.
  2. Keep the boat close: the quick-stop (head up immediately, tack, leave the jib backed, circle back) keeps you within a few boat lengths.
  3. Approach on a close reach so you can slow down by easing sails, and stop with the person to leeward, alongside the cockpit, so the boat drifts toward them and doesn't blanket them in its wind.
  4. Get a line to them right away, then work out how to lift them. Getting someone aboard is often harder than getting back to them.
Lifesling
A floating horseshoe collar on a long line, stowed at the stern rail. Deploy it, then sail in circles around the person until the line reaches them. They put the sling on under their arms; you stop the boat, pull them alongside, and hoist with a halyard and tackle or a winch.
Swim ladder
Only useful if the person can still climb. Cold or injured people often can't.
Under power
Approach slowly and put the engine in neutral well before you reach the person. Never come near anyone in the water with the prop turning.

Practice with a fender or a bucket on a calm day, with each person on board taking the helm.

Heaving to

Heaving to parks a sailboat in a steady, comfortable position, moving slowly forward and drifting to leeward. Use it to reef, eat, fix something, wait for a squall to pass, or handle an emergency.

  1. Sail close-hauled.
  2. Tack, but don't release the jib sheet. The jib stays backed on the old side.
  3. Ease the mainsheet partway.
  4. Push the tiller to leeward and lash it there.

The backed jib pushes the bow away from the wind while the main and rudder push it back up, so the boat settles 45° to 60° off the wind making perhaps a knot. Leave plenty of room to leeward. To get going again, release the tiller and the jib and trim normally.

Before you leave the dock

  • Weather: forecast, radar, and any advisories. Decide your limits before you go.
  • Float plan: tell someone ashore where you're going, who's aboard, and when to call for help if you're not back.
  • Boat: bilge dry, through-hulls checked, rigging pins and rings in place, sails and lines ready, fuel and battery checked, engine runs and pumps cooling water.
  • Safety gear: life jackets for everyone (fitted for kids), throwable, horn, fire extinguisher, lights, radio and phone charged, first aid kit, water.
  • Crew briefing: where the life jackets, extinguisher, and first aid kit are; how to use the radio; what to do if someone goes overboard; how to stop the engine; where to sit and what to hold.

Fire, fuel, and running aground

Gasoline vapor
Heavier than air; it sinks into the bilge. Before starting a gasoline inboard, sniff the engine space and run the blower at least 4 minutes.
Fueling
Stop engines, close hatches and ports, no smoking, keep the nozzle touching the fill, don't overfill. Afterward, wipe spills, open up, run the blower, and sniff before starting.
Fire classes
A: wood, paper, cloth. B: flammable liquids like fuel and oil. C: electrical. Marine extinguishers are usually B or B-C.
Using an extinguisher
PASS: Pull the pin, Aim at the base of the fire, Squeeze, Sweep side to side.
Running aground
Check first for leaks or injury. Then try to back off the way you came. On a keelboat, heel the boat to reduce draft: crew to leeward, boom swung out with someone on it. You can also kedge off by setting an anchor in deep water and pulling. On a falling lake or tide, act fast.

Cold water and heat

1-10-1. After a fall into cold water: about 1 minute of gasping cold shock (keep your head above water and get your breathing under control), about 10 minutes of useful movement before your arms and hands stop working, and about 1 hour before hypothermia makes you unconscious. Wearing a life jacket is what gets you through the first minute.
  • Cold shock is a risk in water below about 60°F, which southern lakes reach in winter and early spring.
  • In the water, keep clothes on, stay still, and pull your knees to your chest (HELP position) or huddle with others to save heat.
  • Hypothermia care: get them out of the water and wind, handle gently, replace wet clothes, insulate, warm the core first. Get medical help for anyone confused or not shivering.
  • Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache. Move to shade, cool down, sip water.
  • Heat stroke is an emergency: confusion, hot skin, possibly no sweating. Cool them immediately with water and call for help.

How a sailboat handles under power

  • No brakes. You stop with reverse, and the boat carries way for a long time. Learn how far your boat coasts in neutral.
  • Steering needs flow. The rudder works only when water moves past it. At very low speed you have little control, especially in reverse.
  • Prop wash. A short burst of forward throttle with the tiller over pushes water across the rudder and kicks the stern sideways, even before the boat picks up speed.
  • Prop walk. In reverse, a propeller pulls the stern sideways. With a right-hand prop, the most common kind on inboards, the stern walks to port in reverse. Find out which way your boat walks by backing up in open water. Use it: dock on the side your stern walks toward.
  • Outboards, common on lake keelboats, have little prop walk and can be turned to steer, but the rudder and outboard must work together.
  • Pivot point. A sailboat turns around a point near its keel, so the stern swings out as the bow turns. Watch the stern near docks and other boats.
  • Windage. In wind, the bow blows downwind quickly when you're going slowly. Keep enough speed to steer, and expect the bow to fall off.

Coming alongside a dock

  1. Rig fenders and dock lines on the docking side before you get close, and brief the crew on who handles which line.
  2. Check the wind and current. Approach into whichever is stronger so it helps you stop.
  3. Approach slowly at a shallow angle, about 20° to 30°. "Never approach a dock faster than you're willing to hit it."
  4. Shift to neutral early and coast in. A touch of reverse stops you (and walks the stern).
  5. Crew steps, never jumps, onto the dock with the midships or bow line and takes a turn around a cleat.
Wind blowing onto the dock
Easy: stop parallel to the dock a few feet off and let the wind push you in. Don't come in at an angle or the bow will hit first.
Wind blowing off the dock
Harder: come in at a steeper angle and a bit faster, get the bow or midships line on fast, then motor gently against a spring to bring the stern in.

Never put hands, feet, or bodies between the boat and the dock. Let fenders take the load.

Dock lines and springs

Bow line
From the bow forward to the dock. Holds the bow in.
Stern line
From the stern aft to the dock. Holds the stern in.
Spring lines
Run diagonally. A spring from the bow area leading aft to the dock stops the boat moving forward; one from the stern area leading forward stops it moving aft.
Breast line
Straight across from boat to dock; holds the boat close. Usually temporary.
  • Leave some slack for wind and waves (and in tidal water, enough for the tide's range).
  • Secure to the dock with a cleat hitch or a bowline over a piling; adjust from on board.
  • Chafe guards where lines pass over edges.

Leaving the dock: springing off

When the wind pins you to the dock, use a spring line to swing one end out first.

Stern out
Rig a spring from the bow cleat leading aft to the dock, with a fender at the bow. Release the other lines, then motor slowly forward with the tiller turned to push the stern out. The spring holds the bow; the stern swings away from the dock. When it's well out, back away in reverse and release the spring.
Bow out
Rig a spring from the stern leading forward to the dock, with a fender at the stern. Back gently in reverse; the bow swings out. Then motor forward and release.

Rig springs as slip lines: around the dock cleat and back to the boat, so you can release them from on board.

Slips

  • Before entering a marina, decide which slip, which side the lines go on, and whether you'll go in bow or stern first.
  • Line up well outside the fairway so you're straight as you enter. Turn into the slip early enough to allow for the stern swinging out.
  • Backing in is harder because of prop walk and weak steering in reverse; get some sternway going in open water first so the rudder bites.
  • Have a crew member ready forward with a boat hook and a line to drop over a piling, and someone aft to stop the boat with a stern line if needed.
  • If an approach goes wrong, abort early: back out or go around and try again. There's no prize for saving a bad approach.

Under sail and picking up a mooring

  • Under sail, plan to stop by heading into the wind. Know your boat's carry, how far it coasts head to wind.
  • Make the final approach on a close reach so you can control speed by easing and trimming, as in a crew-overboard recovery.
  • Under sail, the leeward side of a dock (wind blowing you away from it) is safer: if you misjudge, the wind carries you off rather than slamming you in.
  • Mooring: approach slowly into the wind (or current, if stronger), aiming to stop with the pickup buoy at the bow. A crew member points at it the whole time and picks up the pennant with a boat hook. Secure it to the bow cleat.
  • Under power, keep the buoy on the side the helmsman can see and go to neutral before the pennant reaches the prop.

Anchoring, in depth

Ground tackle
Anchor plus rode. Rode is usually some chain (resists chafe, adds weight) plus nylon line (stretches to absorb shock). Secure the bitter end to the boat.
Anchor types
Fluke (Danforth style): light, strong in sand and mud, common on lake boats. Plow and scoop types (CQR, Delta, Rocna): reset well when the wind shifts. Claw (Bruce): sets easily, holds less for its weight. Mushroom: permanent moorings.
Scope
Rode length ÷ (depth + bow height). About 7:1 normally, 5:1 in calm conditions with chain, 10:1 in a blow.
Swing room
You'll swing in a circle with radius about equal to your rode. Keep that clear of other boats, docks, and shallows.
  1. Choose a spot with good holding, protection from the wind, and room to swing. Check the depth and bottom on the chart.
  2. Approach head to wind and stop. Lower the anchor to the bottom.
  3. Let the boat fall back as you pay out rode. Snub it at about half scope to help it bite, then pay out the rest.
  4. Set it by backing down gently, then more firmly, while watching a range on shore. If the range doesn't change, you're holding.
  5. Keep an anchor watch: check landmarks or a GPS anchor alarm, especially when the wind changes.
  6. To leave, motor slowly toward the anchor while the crew takes in rode. When the boat is directly over it, the anchor breaks out. Don't pull the boat forward by the rode alone.

Apparent wind

The wind you feel on a moving boat is the apparent wind: the true wind combined with the "wind" made by your own motion. Sails, telltales, and the masthead fly all respond to apparent wind.

  • It always comes from farther forward than the true wind (except dead downwind).
  • Sailing upwind it feels stronger than the true wind; sailing downwind it feels weaker. A run can feel gentle while the true wind is strong, so be careful turning upwind.
  • When the boat speeds up, the apparent wind moves forward and you can trim in. When a puff hits, the apparent wind moves aft for a moment, so you can head up a little.

Sail shape

Draft (depth)
How deep the curve of the sail is. A fuller sail gives more power for light air and chop; a flatter sail gives less drag and heel for strong wind.
Draft position
Where the deepest part sits, ideally about 40% to 50% back from the luff. Wind pushes it aft; tensioning the luff pulls it forward.
Twist
How much the top of the sail opens (falls off to leeward) compared with the bottom. More twist spills wind and depowers; less twist powers up and points higher.
Leech
A tight leech means little twist; an open leech means more.

Reading telltales

Jib luff telltales
Both sides streaming = trimmed right. Windward one lifting or fluttering: you're too close to the wind or the sail is too loose. Bear away or trim in. Leeward one fluttering: sail is stalled. Head up or ease. Steering rule: move the tiller toward the fluttering telltale.
Top vs bottom
If the top telltales break first, the jib has too much twist: move the jib lead (fairlead car) forward. If the bottom ones break first, move it aft. Aim for top and bottom breaking together.
Main leech telltales
Ribbons on the leech should mostly stream aft. If the top one keeps curling behind the sail, the leech is too tight: ease the mainsheet or vang, or drop the traveler.
Masthead fly (Windex)
Shows the apparent wind direction at the top of the mast. Useful downwind to avoid sailing by the lee.

Power up, power down

Light air
Fuller sails: ease outhaul, halyard, and backstay. Traveler up to windward to bring the boom to the centerline without over-tightening the leech. Some twist. Keep the boat moving; don't pinch. Crew to leeward to help the sails fill.
Medium air
Full power. Leech telltales streaming, jib telltales flowing, boat at moderate heel.
Heavy air
Flatten (outhaul, halyard or cunningham, backstay), then drop the traveler, then add twist and ease the main in gusts. Crew weight on the rail. When that's not enough, reef.
Depower in this order: flatten the sails, drop the traveler, twist and ease in the puffs, then reef. Reef the main before the jib on most sloops.

Heel and helm

  • Most keelboats sail fastest at moderate heel, roughly 15° to 20°. Beyond that, the boat makes more leeway, weather helm builds, and speed drops.
  • Weather helm (boat wants to round up) increases with heel and with an over-trimmed main. A few degrees of weather helm is good: it gives feel and lift from the rudder. Lots of it means too much sail or too much heel.
  • To reduce weather helm: ease the main or drop the traveler, flatten the main, reef the main, or move crew weight to windward.
  • Lee helm (boat wants to bear away) usually appears in very light air or with too much jib. Trim the main in a little, or ease the jib.

Shifts and puffs

Header
A shift that moves the wind forward, so close-hauled you have to bear away. The jib luffs.
Lift
A shift that moves the wind aft, so you can head up.
Working shifts upwind
Sail the lifts, and tack on the headers: a header on one tack is a lift on the other.
Feathering
Heading up slightly in a gust upwind so the jib just starts to luff, which reduces heel without losing speed.
Puffs
Dark patches on the water. Ease the main or feather as one hits; trim back in as it passes.

On lakes, shifts are frequent and big. Keep looking upwind for dark water and at boats ahead to see what's coming.

Downwind

  • Ease the sails out until they just start to luff, then trim in slightly. On a run, the main goes nearly to the shrouds.
  • Ease the vang off a reach a little to let the main breathe, but keep enough on to stop the boom lifting and the top of the sail twisting off.
  • Wing-and-wing: on a run, set the jib on the opposite side from the main. A whisker pole holds the jib out steadily.
  • A broad reach is often faster and safer than a dead run, and less likely to cause an accidental jibe.
  • A preventer (a line from the boom end forward to the bow and back to the cockpit) holds the boom out to stop an accidental jibe in waves.
  • Watch the masthead fly and the jib: if the jib starts to collapse and fill on the other side, you're close to sailing by the lee.
